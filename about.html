About the project

How we built the project

This page documents the three levels of the digital humanities workflow behind the site: sources, processing, and presentation. It also explains the reasoning behind our design and editorial decisions, along with team roles, bios, and acknowledgments.

Project frame
  • Primary dataset: EPA Smart Location Database, joined with ACS context.
  • Geographic scale: LA County census block groups.
  • Core question: how history, access, and environmental burden shape walkability.
  • Presentation strategy: narrative-first site with charts, map, and timeline.
Sources

Level 1

Our core dataset is the EPA Smart Location Database (SLD), which calculates the 2019 National Walkability Index at the census block-group level. We paired the SLD with ACS demographic and income variables, then used TIGER/Line geometries for mapping. Supporting context came from the SLD technical documentation, the National Walkability Index user guide, planning history, and scholarship on heat, asthma, parks, segregation, and perceived walkability.

We chose these sources because they work at compatible geographic scales and let us hold two things together: the quantifiable built environment and the historical or social forces that the dataset alone does not explain. The EPA data tells us where a neighborhood sits inside a formal walkability metric. The outside scholarship tells us why those patterns matter and what the metric leaves out.

Processing

Level 2

We used Python, pandas, and geopandas to clean the LA-focused CSV, normalize county codes, filter to LA County, compute deciles and correlations, and export a simplified GeoJSON for the interactive map. Visualization assets were generated as reproducible SVGs and PNGs rather than hand-edited screenshots. This kept the charts tied to the actual data and made revision easier.

Our main processing decision was to keep the block group as the analytical unit. We did not collapse the county into tract- or city-level averages because that would erase variation inside neighborhoods and obscure the uneven geography of access. We also joined in demographic context because the native SLD ontology overemphasizes jobs, land-use efficiency, and transit proximity while omitting many forms of inequality that shape everyday mobility.

Presentation

Level 3

The site is organized around narrative movement rather than dashboard logic. The argument starts with a map and charts, then places those patterns inside a timeline and a longer written interpretation. We used React and Tailwind for the main page, Leaflet for the interactive map, and a hosted TimelineJS embed for historical context. The supporting pages separate methods, critique, and bibliography so readers can move between argument and documentation.

Visually, we avoided a dark technical interface because the project is about the civic and human stakes of the built environment, not only its metrics. The palette borrows from LA’s atmosphere: warm sunset tones, pale sand, and clear blue accents. We kept the layout restrained and readable, closer to a publication or exhibit than to a monitoring dashboard.

Reasoning and Class Context

Our workflow follows a critical data humanities approach rather than a purely technical one. We were guided especially by Catherine D'Ignazio and Lauren Klein's argument in Data Feminism that data practice should account for power, omission, and unequal visibility. That perspective shaped our decision to foreground variables connected to income, car ownership, and neighborhood burden instead of treating the walkability index as self-sufficient.

We were also influenced by danah boyd and Kate Crawford's warning that large datasets can appear socially complete even when they reproduce selective ways of seeing. The SLD is rich in land-use and accessibility metrics, but it cannot capture fear, comfort, disability access, or historical dispossession on its own. Our narrative and critique pages therefore emphasize what the dataset can do and what it cannot do.

Finally, our chart design decisions draw on Cole Nussbaumer Knaflic's emphasis on decluttering and story structure. Instead of showing every possible relationship, we selected charts that answer a specific question in sequence: where walkability is concentrated, how it relates to car ownership and transit access, how it intersects with low-wage labor and income, and how those findings sit inside a longer history of planning and inequality.

Rubric Coverage

Core requirements
  • Narrative: main argument and visual story on the narrative page, with more than 2,000 words across the story sections and long-form interpretation.
  • Timeline: embedded Knight Lab timeline on the timeline section.
  • Map: interactive Leaflet choropleth on the map section.
  • Five or more visualizations: histogram, income quartile boxplot, two scatterplots, decile comparison chart, and correlation heatmap on the main site.
  • Annotated bibliography: dedicated page at Bibliography.
Supporting requirements
  • About page: sources, processing, presentation, decision rationale, class readings, team bios, responsibilities, and acknowledgments on this page.
  • Data critique: dedicated page at Data Critique covering ontology, exclusions, interpretive limits, and ideological effects.
  • Illustrations and captions: every chart and map on the main page includes figure captions and source framing.
  • Citations: linked MLA-style citations are included in the narrative and expanded on the bibliography page.
  • Humanities framing: the project treats walkability as a historical and political condition, not only a technical metric.
