Data critique

What the dataset can show, and what it cannot

Our project uses the EPA Smart Location Database because it provides a strong national walkability measure at the census block-group level. At the same time, its structure privileges some ways of seeing Los Angeles and obscures others. This page explains the dataset’s contents, generation, limits, and ideological effects.

Dataset core
  • Unit of analysis: 2019 census block groups.
  • Main index: National Walkability Index.
  • Supplementary fields: households, income, employment, car ownership, transit, density.
  • Joined context: ACS demographic variables used in the LA County analysis.

What is included

The Smart Location Database contains detailed spatial and socioeconomic information for census block groups across the United States. It includes population and household counts, income-related fields, car ownership, employment by sector, destination accessibility, transit access, and variables tied to the “five Ds” of urban form: density, diversity, design, destination accessibility, and distance to transit. For this project, those variables are useful because they allow us to ask how walkability is distributed across LA County and how it relates to infrastructure, labor geography, and household constraints.

In our LA County workflow, the SLD is joined to ACS variables that add race and ethnicity, education, age structure, and housing burden. That supplemental join is important. Without it, the native SLD would remain far more legible as a land-use database than as a social inequality database. The joined version lets us pose humanities questions about privilege, exposure, and uneven access rather than only technical questions about built-form efficiency.

How the data was generated

The SLD is not a single-source dataset. It is an assembled product funded through the EPA’s Smart Growth Program and developed with support from the U.S. General Services Administration. The 2019 block-group framework is combined with Census and ACS products, LEHD employment data, TIGER/Line geometries, street-network and land-use data, and transit feeds from multiple agencies and shared GTFS inventories. The walkability index itself is then constructed from a weighted combination of intersection density, proximity to transit, employment mix, and employment-plus-household mix.

That origin matters because it reveals the dataset’s intended use. The SLD was created as a planning and smart-growth resource, not as a community ethnography. It is optimized for national consistency, comparability, and planning relevance. Those priorities explain both the dataset’s strengths and its blind spots.

What the dataset can illuminate

The dataset is good at revealing broad structural relationships. It can show where jobs, destinations, and transit cluster. It can compare car-free household share to transit access. It can show whether high walkability tends to coincide with labor concentration, compact urban form, and mixed use. It can also reveal where LA County departs from a simple center-periphery model. For example, some highly walkable neighborhoods are not the highest income neighborhoods, which complicates assumptions that urban access maps directly onto neighborhood wealth.

Because the SLD works at block-group scale, it is especially useful for mapping intra-county variation. That scale lets us treat LA County not as a single urban landscape but as a set of unevenly connected mobility environments. It also supports comparison between infrastructure access and household reliance on walking or transit. These are exactly the kinds of spatial relationships a digital humanities project can turn into a narrative about everyday life, governance, and unequal opportunity.

What the dataset cannot reveal

The dataset does not measure walking behavior itself. There are no pedestrian counts, daily walking frequencies, step counts, or distinctions between walking for errands, work, recreation, or school. A block group can score highly on the walkability index and still contain routes that are unpleasant or unsafe to use. Likewise, a neighborhood can support frequent walking for social or economic reasons even if its environmental proxies are weaker.

The SLD also omits many conditions that residents experience immediately: sidewalk quality, curb ramps, traffic speed, shade, hilliness, disability access, lighting, police presence, air pollution, and neighborhood fear. Those absences mean that the SLD can describe the morphology of opportunity more clearly than the lived texture of walking. This is one reason we pair it with scholarship on asthma, urban heat, park access, and perceived walkability.

What would be left out without outside sources

Without external demographic joins and historical interpretation, the SLD would leave out many of the inequalities we care about most. We would know where jobs and transit are concentrated, but not how race, ethnicity, education, age, and housing burden shape the ability to benefit from those conditions. We would see infrastructure form more clearly than labor conditions, undocumented or informal work, or exposure to pollution and unsafe streets.

We would also lose historical causation. The dataset does not tell us why some areas were disconnected, rezoned, industrialized, or divided by freeways. Those stories come from planning history, environmental justice research, and community-focused scholarship, not from the spreadsheet itself.

Ontology and ideological effects

The dataset’s ontology reflects the values of smart-growth planning. It treats walkability as a problem of measurable urban design: connectivity, proximity, destination access, and efficient movement. That approach is analytically useful, but it also carries an ideological effect. It encourages readers to understand social problems spatially and to imagine that they can be solved through design, transit proximity, and land use optimization alone.

In practice, that framing can sideline politics, history, and unequal exposure. A neighborhood may rank well because it is dense, mixed-use, and close to jobs while still being dangerous to walk in, thermally punishing, or burdened by pollution. Metrics like jobs reachable within forty-five minutes emphasize efficiency, productivity, and regional access, but they do not ask whether those jobs are secure, well paid, safe, or accessible to all workers on equal terms.

This project therefore treats the SLD as a valuable but incomplete representation. It is strongest when used as one layer in a larger interpretive frame. It becomes misleading when treated as a comprehensive measure of neighborhood life.

Research questions shaped by the critique

  • How well do walkability scores reflect and affect economic disparities in Los Angeles?
  • How well served by public transportation are neighborhoods with low rates of car ownership?
  • How do heat, pollution, and asthma complicate what the index calls a walkable place?
  • What does the absence of race in the native SLD make harder to see?
  • To what extent do walkability scores reflect long histories of investment and divestment in Los Angeles?
  • What problems arise when environmental proxies stand in for walking behavior itself?
