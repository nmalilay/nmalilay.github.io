Annotated bibliography

Works cited and how we used them

Citations are presented in MLA style and grouped by how they inform the project. The annotations explain why each source matters for the site’s argument about history, infrastructure, inequality, and lived pedestrian experience in Los Angeles.

Scope
  • Methodological and measurement sources.
  • LA-specific planning and mobility scholarship.
  • Environmental justice, heat, and health sources.
  • Historical planning documents used in the narrative and timeline.

Measurement and Method

Adkins, Arlie, et al. “Contextualizing Walkability: Do Relationships Between Built Environments and Walking Vary by Socioeconomic Context?” Journal of the American Planning Association, vol. 83, no. 3, 2017, pp. 296-314, https://doi.org/10.1080/01944363.2017.1322527.

This source is central to our claim that walkability should not be interpreted the same way across all neighborhoods. Adkins and colleagues show that built-form indicators interact with socioeconomic context, which helps us argue that a strong score can still mask unequal access or unequal burden.

He, Xuan, and Sylvia Y. He. “Using Mobile Phone Big Data and Street View Images to Explore the Mismatch Between Walkability and Walking Behavior.” Transportation Research Part A: Policy and Practice, vol. 180, 2024, p. 103946, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tra.2023.103946.

We use this article to show that walkability scores and walking behavior do not always align. It supports our critique of the SLD as a proxy-based dataset and points toward future methods that integrate actual pedestrian behavior rather than relying only on environmental form.

Ki, Donghwan, et al. “Measuring Nuanced Walkability: Leveraging ChatGPT’s Vision Reasoning With Multisource Spatial Data.” Computers, Environment and Urban Systems, vol. 121, 2025, p. 102319, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compenvurbsys.2025.102319.

This source helps us frame the future of walkability research. It is useful because it suggests ways of capturing nuanced street-level conditions that are largely absent from the Smart Location Database, including visual and experiential features that matter to pedestrians.

Van Der Vlugt, Anna-Lena, et al. “Analysing the Determinants of Perceived Walkability, and Its Effects on Walking.” Transportation Research Part A: Policy and Practice, vol. 197, 2025, p. 104498, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tra.2025.104498.

We cite this article to distinguish perceived walkability from objective walkability. That distinction matters for our project because the SLD measures environments structurally, while many residents experience walkability through safety, comfort, and neighborhood feel.

Los Angeles Planning and Mobility

Banerjee, Tridib, et al. “Walking to School: The Experience of Children in Inner City Los Angeles and Implications for Policy.” Journal of Planning Education and Research, vol. 34, no. 2, 2014, pp. 123-140, https://doi.org/10.1177/0739456x14522494.

This article grounds our narrative in lived mobility rather than abstract design. It shows that walking in Los Angeles is shaped by traffic, safety, and neighborhood disorder, which helps us argue that infrastructure access alone does not fully describe pedestrian experience.

Lee, Jeongwoo. “Impact of Neighborhood Walkability on Trip Generation and Trip Chaining: Case of Los Angeles.” Journal of Urban Planning and Development, vol. 142, no. 3, 2015, https://doi.org/10.1061/(asce)up.1943-5444.0000312.

We use Lee’s work to connect neighborhood walkability to actual travel patterns in Los Angeles. The article is especially useful for showing that walkable residential environments can reduce vehicle dependence and affect how people chain daily trips.

Horak, Katie, et al. Historical Housing and Land Use Study. City of Los Angeles Department of City Planning, 20 Sept. 2024, https://planning.lacity.gov/plans-policies/community-plan-update/housing-element-rezoning-program-news/historical-housing-and.

This report is one of the project’s key historical sources. It helps us connect present-day spatial patterns to land-use policy, exclusionary zoning, and uneven investment over time. The report is especially important for the site’s timeline and for the argument that walkability is historically produced.

Fisher, John E. Transportation Topics and Tales: Milestones in Transportation History in Southern California. Los Angeles Department of Transportation, https://ladot.lacity.gov/sites/default/files/documents/transportation-topics-and-tales-milestones-in-transportation-history-in-southern-california.pdf.

We use this LADOT document to anchor the transportation history portion of the timeline, especially the rise of the freeway era. It helps frame regional mobility decisions as part of a longer institutional history rather than as isolated infrastructure choices.

Equity, Environment, and Public Health

Ki, Donghwan, and Zhenhua Chen. “Walkability Inequity in Los Angeles: Uncovering the Overlooked Role of Micro-level Features.” Transportation Research Part D: Transport and Environment, vol. 122, 2023, p. 103888, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.trd.2023.103888.

This is the most directly relevant scholarly source in the bibliography because it focuses on Los Angeles and on the inequities missed by coarse built-environment measures. It gives us strong evidence that micro-scale features can worsen walkability in minority neighborhoods even when larger urban-form conditions seem favorable.

Rigolon, Alessandro, et al. “Who Has More Walkable Routes to Parks? An Environmental Justice Study of Safe Routes to Parks in Neighborhoods of Los Angeles.” Journal of Urban Affairs, vol. 40, no. 4, 2018, pp. 576-591, https://doi.org/10.1080/07352166.2017.1360740.

Rigolon and coauthors show that park access is not only about distance; it is also about the quality and safety of the route. We use this source to support the claim that neighborhoods may appear accessible on paper while still providing unequal pedestrian conditions in practice.

Spoer, Ben R., et al. “Association between Racial Residential Segregation and Walkability in 745 U.S. Cities.” Health & Place, vol. 84, 2023, article 103114, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.healthplace.2023.103114.

This national-scale study gives us a comparative equity frame beyond Los Angeles. It helps contextualize the possibility that highly walkable environments can still coexist with racial segregation and unequal health outcomes, which complicates easy interpretations of “good” urban form.

Kim, Yonsu, et al. “The Built Environment and Asthma: Los Angeles Case Study.” Journal of Public Health, vol. 31, no. 1, 2023, pp. 57-64, https://doi.org/10.1007/s10389-020-01417-6.

We cite this study to show that the built environment is inseparable from health outcomes. It extends the project’s argument beyond transportation by showing that neighborhood form and environmental conditions affect respiratory vulnerability in Los Angeles.

Engel, Ruth A., et al. “Contributions of Roads to Surface Temperature: Evidence from Southern California.” Environmental Research Communications, vol. 5, no. 1, 2022, https://doi.org/10.1088/2515-7620/acabb8.

This article is important for the project’s climate dimension. It helps us explain why a technically walkable route can still be environmentally hostile when road surfaces, shade loss, and heat exposure make walking unsafe or exhausting.

Vahmani, P., and G. A. Ban-Weiss. “Impact of Remotely Sensed Albedo and Vegetation Fraction on Simulation of Urban Climate in WRF-Urban Canopy Model: A Case Study of the Urban Heat Island in Los Angeles.” Journal of Geophysical Research: Atmospheres, vol. 121, no. 4, 2016, pp. 1511-1531, https://doi.org/10.1002/2015JD023718.

We use this source alongside Engel et al. to show that vegetation, urban fraction, and environmental design shape how the city feels on foot. It strengthens the claim that walkability must be interpreted alongside urban heat and public health, not only as a mobility score.

Fraser, Andrew M., et al. “Household Accessibility to Heat Refuges: Residential Air Conditioning, Public Cooled Space, and Walkability.” Environment and Planning B: Urban Analytics and City Science, vol. 44, no. 6, 2017, pp. 1036-1055, https://doi.org/10.1177/0265813516657342.

This source broadens the climate angle by tying walkability to access to heat refuges and cooling infrastructure. It is useful because it reframes walkability as part of a broader survival geography under heat stress rather than only a convenience metric.
